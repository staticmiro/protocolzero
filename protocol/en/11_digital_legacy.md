# 📖 PART XI:       DIGITAL LEGACY

## 42. Digital Will: "Just in Case" Plan

Death is certain.   Timing is not.  

Your digital life needs a plan for when you're gone. 

### Why This Isn't Morbid, It's Responsible

**You have digital assets:**

- Photos and videos (family memories)
- Documents (financial, legal, personal)
- Accounts (email, social media, banking)
- Subscriptions (ongoing charges)
- Cryptocurrency (if applicable)
- Domain names, websites
- Digital purchases (books, music, games)
- Creative work (writing, art, code)

**Without instructions, family faces:**

**Locked accounts:**  
No passwords, no access, no way to retrieve memories or critical documents.

**Financial loss:**  
Subscriptions continue charging.    Cryptocurrency lost forever.   Assets unknown and unclaimed.

**Legal complications:**  
Proving ownership of digital assets.  Accessing accounts without credentials. 

**Emotional burden:**  
Grief compounded by frustration of locked-out accounts containing precious memories.

**Identity theft:**  
Accounts remain active, vulnerable to compromise after your death.

**Planning for digital death is responsible care for those you leave behind.**

### **[CRITICAL]** What Digital Will Should Contain

**Inventory of accounts:**

Complete list: 
- Email accounts
- Social media
- Banking and financial
- Investment and retirement
- Insurance
- Subscriptions
- Cloud storage
- Domain names and hosting
- Cryptocurrency wallets
- Gaming accounts
- Loyalty programs
- Any account with value (financial or sentimental)

For each account: 
- Service name
- Username/email
- Where password is stored (password manager, etc.)
- Whether you want it closed, memorialized, or accessed

**Access instructions:**

**Password manager:**
- Master password location
- Recovery codes location
- How to access

**Email:**
- Primary email password or how to access it
- Recovery information

**Devices:**
- PINs/passwords for phone, computer, tablet
- Biometric backups (obviously won't work after death, need alternative)

**Two-factor authentication:**
- Recovery codes location
- Backup authentication methods

**Encryption keys:**
- Location of any encryption keys for important files
- Instructions for decryption

**Instructions for specific accounts:**

**Delete:**  
Accounts you want closed and data removed.

**Memorialize:**  
Social media accounts to be converted to memorial (if service offers this).

**Access and manage:**  
Accounts family should access to retrieve information or manage affairs.

**Transfer:**  
Domain names, websites, creative works with instructions for transfer.

**Financial information:**

**Assets:**
- Bank accounts
- Investment accounts
- Cryptocurrency (wallet addresses, seed phrase locations)
- Pending payments owed to you

**Liabilities:**
- Subscriptions to cancel
- Debts
- Recurring charges

**Location of documents:**

- Legal will
- Insurance policies
- Property deeds
- Tax records
- Financial statements
- Medical directives

**Digital or physical locations** where these are stored. 

**Contact information:**

People to notify:
- Lawyer
- Financial advisor
- Accountant
- Employer
- Close friends

**Special instructions:**

- What to do with creative work (publish, keep private, destroy)
- Specific messages for family members
- Distribution of digital assets
- Privacy preferences (what should remain private even after death)

### **[RECOMMENDED]** Format:  Paper, Digital, Legal

**Paper (recommended for critical information):**

Physical document stored securely. 

Pros:
- Survives digital device failures
- Accessible without passwords
- Can be stored in safe, safety deposit box, with lawyer
- Legally valid in most jurisdictions

Cons:
- Can be lost, damaged, destroyed
- Must be updated manually
- Security risk if not stored properly

**Digital (for detailed instructions):**

Encrypted file with comprehensive instructions.

Pros:
- Easy to update
- Can include extensive detail
- Can include links, screenshots, step-by-step guides
- Can be backed up

Cons:
- Requires access instructions (password/key)
- Vulnerable if encryption key is lost
- Format may become obsolete

**Legal will (for binding instructions):**

Digital assets included in formal legal will.

Pros:
- Legally enforceable
- Clear ownership and transfer instructions
- Attorney-guided to ensure validity

Cons:
- Expensive
- Less flexible (formal amendments required for changes)
- Publicly accessible after probate in many jurisdictions
- May not cover all digital assets adequately

**Recommended approach:   Combination.**

**Legal will:**  
High-level.    "Digital assets to be distributed according to instructions in digital estate plan stored at [location]."

**Paper document:**  
Critical passwords, master keys, encryption keys, locations.    Stored securely.

**Digital document:**  
Detailed instructions, account lists, specific wishes.    Encrypted, with decryption key in paper document.

### How Often to Update

**Digital life changes constantly.  **  Digital will needs regular updates. 

**Update frequency:**

**Annually (minimum):**  
Review entire document.   Update accounts, passwords (if stored), instructions.

**Major life events:**
- Marriage, divorce, separation
- Birth of children
- Death of designated executor/beneficiary
- Moving
- New job
- Significant financial changes
- New significant digital assets (cryptocurrency, business)

**Regular maintenance:**
- New important account created
- Account closed
- Password manager changed
- Major password changes
- Encryption methods changed

**Set calendar reminder:**  
Same time each year (birthday, new year, specific date).

**Review takes 30-60 minutes.**  Small time investment for significant peace of mind.

**Test the process:**

Periodically ask:   "If I died today, could my family follow these instructions?"

Test with trusted person (spouse, adult child):  
Can they access what they need with your instructions?   (Don't give them actual access, just walk through the process.)

### Common Mistakes

**No digital will at all**  
"I'm young, I'll do it later."   Accidents happen.   Do it now.

**Storing access information in only one place**  
Single copy lost/destroyed = family locked out.    Redundancy essential.

**Instructions too vague**  
"My passwords are in a safe place."   Where?   What safe place?

**Never updating**  
Document from 10 years ago references accounts that no longer exist, omits current accounts.

**Giving full access to someone you no longer trust**  
Divorce, falling out, relationship changes.   Update beneficiaries and executors.

**Including full passwords in will**  
Will becomes public record in probate.   Include location of passwords, not passwords themselves.

**Not telling anyone document exists**  
Perfect plan hidden in safety deposit box nobody knows about.   Tell executor where to find it.

**Digital-only with no backup**  
Single digital file corrupted or on encrypted drive with lost password. 

**Too complicated**  
100-page document with technical jargon.   Grieving family won't follow complex instructions.   Keep it clear and actionable.

### Quick Wins

- **1 hour:** Create basic digital will document listing major accounts and passwords location. 
- **30 minutes:** Store paper copy in secure location, tell trusted person where it is.
- **15 minutes:** Set annual calendar reminder to review and update. 
- **20 minutes:** Document password manager access instructions for family. 


## 43. Emergency Access for Loved Ones

Beyond death, temporary incapacity can require others to access your accounts.

### **[RECOMMENDED]** Platform Legacy Features

Many services now offer features for account access after death or incapacity. 

**Common features:**

**Inactive account manager:**

If account inactive for specified period (3, 6, 12, 18 months), automated process triggers: 
- Notification sent to designated contacts
- Access granted to specified data
- Account closed (if you chose that option)

**Setup:**
- Designate trusted contacts
- Choose timeout period
- Specify what data they can access
- Provide their contact information

**Legacy contact:**

Person you designate to manage your account after death.

**Permissions vary by service:**
- Memorial mode conversion
- Limited data access
- Account closure
- No access to private messages (usually)

**Setup:**
- Nominate legacy contact
- Contact receives notification (or doesn't, depending on service)
- Contact must provide proof of death to activate

**Digital legacy program:**

Download and transfer of data to designated person.

**Setup:**
- Designate beneficiaries
- Specify what data can be accessed
- Provide contact information
- Beneficiaries must prove your death and their identity

**Enable these features on:**
- Email providers
- Cloud storage
- Social media
- Photo storage
- Any service with important data or memories

### Password Manager Emergency Features

**Password managers offer emergency access:**

**How it works:**

**Emergency contact designation:**
- You designate trusted person
- They request emergency access
- Request triggers waiting period (24-48 hours typically)
- If you don't deny request during waiting period, access is granted
- They receive temporary access to your vault

**Why waiting period:**

Prevents abuse.   

If emergency contact compromised or malicious:  you receive notification, deny request, revoke their emergency status.

If you're truly incapacitated or deceased:   you can't deny, access granted after waiting period.

**Setup:**

1. Choose emergency contact (spouse, adult child, trusted friend)
2. Designate in password manager settings
3. Set waiting period (balance urgency vs security)
4. Contact receives invitation to accept role
5. Test the process (optional but recommended)

**Alternative:  Sealed envelope.**

Write master password on paper, seal in envelope, give to trusted person with instructions:   "Open only if I'm incapacitated or deceased."

Lower tech, but works. 

### Cloud Account Options

**Similar to platform legacy features.**

**Cloud storage legacy access:**

Family can request data after providing death certificate. 

**Account recovery:**

Some services allow designated recovery contacts who can help you regain access if locked out.

Can be used by family after death with proper documentation.

**Family sharing:**

While alive, share specific folders/files with family.

After death, they still have access to shared content (though not entire account).

**Preparation:**

**Create shared folder:**  "Important Documents" or "Family Archive."

Include:
- Scanned legal documents
- Financial records
- Photos and videos
- Instructions

Share with spouse/family. 

If you die, they still have access to this shared content regardless of account access.

### Common Mistakes

**Not enabling legacy features**  
Features exist, nobody uses them.   Enable while you can.

**Designating wrong person**  
Not trustworthy, not tech-savvy enough, estranged family member. 

**Designating same person for everything**  
Single point of failure.    If that person is deceased, incapacitated, or untrustworthy, you have no backup.

**Never testing emergency access**  
Set it up, assume it works.   Doesn't work when needed.

**Not updating emergency contacts**  
Designated person is ex-spouse or deceased friend. 

**Assuming family can access cloud accounts**  
Without legacy access configured, cloud provider won't grant access even with death certificate (policies vary, but many won't).

**Configuring but not documenting**  
Family doesn't know you set up emergency access or how to activate it.

**Choosing waiting period too long**  
7-day waiting period when 48 hours would be sufficient.   Delays urgent access.

### Quick Wins

- **15 minutes:** Enable legacy contact feature on primary email account.
- **10 minutes:** Set up emergency access in password manager.
- **20 minutes:** Configure inactive account manager on major accounts.
- **10 minutes:** Document which services have legacy features enabled and who the contacts are.


## 44. Crisis Scenarios

Planning for specific crises helps family act quickly when they can't ask you. 

### **[CRITICAL]** Memory Loss (Illness, Accident)

**Temporary or permanent inability to remember passwords, accounts, or procedures.**

**Preparation:**

**Physical documentation:**

Critical information on paper, accessible without digital knowledge: 
- List of accounts
- Password manager master password
- Device unlock codes
- Important contacts

Stored where trusted person can find it:   
"Emergency information folder" in known location.

**Simplified access:**

Biometric access on devices (fingerprint, face recognition) works even if you forget passwords.

But family needs backup:   physical password/PIN for when biometrics fail.

**Trusted contacts informed:**

Before crisis:    "If I ever seem confused or can't remember important accounts, check [location] for emergency information."

**Regular practice:**

Recovery processes practiced while you're healthy ensures they work during crisis.

**Medical information included:**

Emergency contacts for: 
- Doctor
- Insurance
- Medical power of attorney
- Medication lists (if stored digitally)

### Death:     Step by Step for Family

**What family should do:**

**Immediate (first 48 hours):**

1. **Locate digital will/emergency instructions**  
   Check known locations (safe, safety deposit box, with lawyer, location you told them about).

2. **Secure devices**  
   Prevent theft or unauthorized access to your devices.

3. **Access password manager**  
   Using master password from emergency instructions.

4. **Access primary email**  
   Critical for account recovery and notifications.

5. **Notify critical services**  
   - Employer
   - Bank (prevent fraud, freeze accounts if needed)
   - payment card companies
   - Insurance companies

**First week:**

6. **Review account inventory**  
   Determine what accounts exist and what to do with each.

7. **Cancel subscriptions**  
   Stop recurring charges.

8. **Memorial settings on social media**  
   Convert to memorial accounts per your instructions.

9. **Access financial accounts**  
   Work with bank and financial institutions to transfer or close. 

10. **Backup important data**  
    Photos, documents, videos from your devices and cloud accounts.

**First month:**

11. **Close unnecessary accounts**  
    Following your instructions about what to delete.

12. **Transfer ownership**  
    Domain names, websites, digital assets to designated recipients.

13. **Handle cryptocurrency**  
    If applicable, access wallets using seed phrases, transfer or convert.

14. **Download data archives**  
    From services before closing accounts.

15. **Monitor for identity theft**  
    Your identity can be stolen after death.   Monitor credit reports, watch for fraudulent accounts.

**Ongoing:**

16. **Final account closures**  
    After data retrieved and transferred.

17. **Document the process**  
    For their own future reference or others in family.

### Disability:    Access for Caregivers

**You're alive but unable to manage your own accounts.**

**Medical, accident, cognitive decline.**

**Preparation requires:**

**Legal documentation:**

Power of attorney (POA):  
Legal authority for someone to act on your behalf.

Should explicitly include digital assets and accounts.

**Accessible instructions:**

Caregiver needs to know:
- What accounts you have
- How to access them
- What needs to be managed (pay bills, access medical records, communicate on your behalf)

**Gradual transition:**

If decline is gradual: 
- Share access progressively
- Add caregiver to accounts as authorized user
- Transition management before you're unable

**Privacy balance:**

Caregiver needs access to functional accounts (banking, medical, utilities).

But may not need access to personal communications, private files. 

Specify what's necessary vs what's private.

**Emergency contacts updated:**

Medical providers, insurance, utilities have caregiver as authorized contact.

### Separation/Divorce:    Separating Access

**Shared digital life during relationship.   Must separate during/after breakup.**

**Immediate actions:**

**Change passwords:**

Every account, especially: 
- Email
- Banking
- Social media
- Cloud storage
- Devices

**Revoke access:**
- Remove from shared accounts
- Remove as legacy contact
- Remove from emergency access
- Revoke device authorization
- Remove from family sharing plans

**Separate accounts:**
- Close joint accounts or remove yourself
- Create new accounts where needed
- Update billing and payment methods

**Update recovery information:**
- Change recovery email and phone
- Change security questions
- Update emergency contacts

**Review shared data:**
- Back up what you want to keep from shared storage
- Remove your data from shared locations
- Stop sharing folders/files

**Social media boundaries:**
- Unfriend/unfollow if appropriate
- Block if situation is hostile
- Remove from tagged photos
- Update relationship status

**Subscriptions and payments:**
- Cancel shared subscriptions or remove your payment method
- Set up individual accounts for services you want to keep

**Change address/location:**

Remove shared address from accounts.  Update to new location.

**Document everything:**

For legal proceedings, document: 
- What accounts existed
- What access was shared
- What you've changed
- Dates of changes

**Legal considerations:**

Some jurisdictions have rules about shared digital assets in divorce.   Consult attorney. 

### Common Mistakes

**No preparation for any crisis scenario**  
"It won't happen to me."   It can. 

**Instructions only in your head**  
Can't communicate during crisis.   Must be documented.

**Family doesn't know where instructions are**  
Perfect plan in location nobody knows about. 

**Instructions require your active participation**  
"Call me to get the password."   What if you can't answer?

**No legal authority for caregiver**  
Without POA, services may refuse to grant access even with good intentions.

**Waiting until crisis to separate accounts**  
Relationship ends badly, you're locked in bitter fight over account access.   Separate proactively.

**Not updating after crisis**  
Crisis passes, you recover, but emergency contacts and access instructions never updated.

**Assuming family can figure it out**  
Technical systems are complex.    Without instructions, family will struggle.

### Quick Wins

- **30 minutes:** Create step-by-step guide for family in case of your death.
- **20 minutes:** Set up power of attorney that includes digital assets (consult attorney).
- **15 minutes:** Document caregiver access procedures in case of disability.
- **Variable:** If separating/divorcing, immediately change critical passwords and revoke shared access.


## 45. Accounts After Death

Services handle death differently.    Some memorialize.   Some delete.  Some lock forever.

### What Happens to Different Types of Accounts

**Email:**

**Typical process:**

Account becomes inactive.    After period (often 3-12 months), may be deleted. 

**With legacy access:** Family can retrieve emails and contacts.

**Without legacy access:**   Usually locked forever.  Company policy often prevents granting access even with death certificate.

**Social media:**

**Options:**
- **Memorial account:**  Profile converted to memorial.   No new posts, but existing content preserved.  
- **Deletion:**  Account fully removed per your instruction. 
- **Inactive then deleted:**  If no instruction, account may eventually be deleted.

**Memorialization process:**

Family submits: 
- Proof of death (death certificate)
- Proof of relationship
- Your wishes (if documented)

Account converted: 
- "Remembering" or "In memory of" label
- No login possible
- Content preserved
- Comments may be disabled or allowed

**Financial accounts:**

**Process:**

Executor of estate contacts bank with death certificate and legal documents. 

Account frozen, then: 
- Funds transferred to estate
- Joint accounts transfer to survivor
- Account closed

**Cannot be accessed without legal authority** (executor, power of attorney, joint owner).

**Subscription services:**

**Continue charging** until someone cancels.

Family needs to:
- Find all subscriptions (payment card statements help)
- Contact each service to cancel
- Request refund for charges after death (often granted)

**Digital purchases (books, music, movies, games):**

**Typically non-transferable.**

Terms of service usually state:  
"License, not ownership.    Non-transferable."

Your library of purchased content dies with you.

Some services may grant exception with death certificate, but most don't have to and won't.

**Domain names and websites:**

**Expire if not renewed.**

Executor can: 
- Renew to keep active
- Transfer to beneficiary
- Let expire

Requires access to registrar account. 

**Cryptocurrency:**

**Access requires private keys/seed phrases.**

Without these:   permanently lost.   No recovery possible.  

With keys:  
- Executor can access wallets
- Transfer funds
- Convert to traditional currency

Include in estate planning.    Otherwise, potentially significant assets vanish.

### **[RECOMMENDED]** Closing vs Memorializing

**Factors to consider:**

**Nature of account:**

**Functional (email, banking, utilities):**   Close after affairs settled.

**Memorial (social media, photo sharing):**   Family may want preserved. 

**Your wishes:**

Some people want digital footprint erased.  
Others want memories preserved. 

**Document your preferences.**

**Family needs:**

Grieving family may find comfort in memorial account where they can visit, leave messages, see photos.

Or may find it painful and prefer closure.

**Discuss with family** if possible.   Their needs matter too.

**Data preservation:**

**Before closing/memorializing:**  Download data archives. 

Once account closed, data may be unrecoverable.

**Recommendation by account type:**

**Social media:**   Memorialize (preserves memories, photos, connections).

**Email:**   Access for estate settlement, then close (or transfer important data, then close).

**Banking/financial:**   Close after estate settled.

**Subscriptions:**   Cancel/close. 

**Cloud storage:**   Download data, then close.

**Websites/blogs:**   Depends.   If public-facing creative work, may want to keep online.  If personal, may want to close.

### Download Data Before Closure

**Critical step before closing any account:**

**Export/download all data.**

**What to download:**

**Photos and videos:**  
Irreplaceable memories.

**Messages and emails:**  
May contain important information or sentimental value.

**Documents:**  
Anything stored in cloud. 

**Contacts:**  
Address books, friend lists. 

**Posts and content:**  
Social media posts, blog entries, creative work.

**Most services offer data export:**

"Download your data" or "Export data" option in settings.

Process generates archive (usually ZIP file) of all your data.

**Download, verify, backup.**

Store in multiple locations.   This is family legacy.

### Preventing Identity Theft After Death

**Deceased persons' identities are stolen.**

**Common frauds:**

- Opening payment cards in deceased's name
- Filing false tax returns
- Taking out loans
- Accessing existing accounts

**Protection steps family should take:**

**1. Notify credit bureaus:**

Report death to major credit bureaus.   Request deceased alert on credit report. 

Prevents new credit from being opened.

**2. Monitor credit reports:**

Even after death, check periodically for fraudulent accounts.

**3. Close or freeze accounts:**

Financial accounts should be closed or frozen.

**4. Notify government agencies:**

Social Security Administration (in US) and equivalent in other countries. 

Prevents fraudulent benefits claims.

**5. Secure documents:**

Death certificate, SSN, and ID documents should be protected. 

Provide copies (not originals) when necessary.

**6. Watch for fraud indicators:**

- Bills for accounts that don't exist
- Collection calls for unknown debts
- Credit denials in deceased's name

**7. Keep some accounts open initially:**

Monitor for fraudulent activity before closing.

Once closed, harder to spot fraud.

**8. Be cautious with obituaries:**

Publishing full name, birthdate, address in obituary provides information for identity thieves.

Consider minimal information in public notices.

### Common Mistakes

**Posting death announcement with full personal details**  
Date of birth, address, mother's maiden name = identity theft gift.

**Closing accounts immediately without downloading data**  
Photos, messages, documents lost forever.

**Not notifying all financial institutions**  
Account remains active, vulnerable to fraud.

**Publishing "RIP" posts with account access info**  
"Dad's password was his birthdate" = invitation to hackers.

**Leaving accounts active indefinitely**  
Charges continue, accounts vulnerable. 

**Not checking for fraudulent activity after death**  
Assume death ends fraud risk.   It doesn't.

**Executor not having legal documentation**  
Services refuse access without proper legal authority.

**Forgetting small accounts**  
Everyone remembers to close bank account.   Forget about streaming services, loyalty programs, niche sites.

### Quick Wins

- **20 minutes:** Document your preferences for each account type (close, memorialize, transfer).
- **30 minutes:** Download data archives from your most important accounts.
- **15 minutes:** Create list of accounts family should close immediately vs monitor vs memorialize.
- **10 minutes:** Store data archives in location family can access (with instructions in digital will).


## 46. Preparing for Neurodegeneration

Gradual cognitive decline presents unique challenges. 

Unlike sudden death or accident, you have time to prepare while you still can.

### Gradual Loss of Capacity

**Neurodegenerative diseases:**

Alzheimer's, dementia, Parkinson's, others cause progressive loss of cognitive function.

**Digital impact:**

Early stages:   Forget passwords occasionally.  
Middle stages:   Forget how to use devices, can't follow complex instructions.  
Late stages:  Cannot interact with technology at all.

**Preparation must happen early** while you're still capable.

### **[CRITICAL]** Simplification Before Illness Progresses

**Reduce complexity while you can still manage it.**

**Consolidate accounts:**

Multiple email accounts → one primary account.  
Multiple banks → one bank.   
Scattered cloud storage → one service. 

Fewer accounts to manage, fewer passwords to remember.

**Document everything:**

**Step-by-step instructions with screenshots:**

"How to check email"  
"How to pay bills online"  
"How to video call family"

Instructions you can follow when you don't remember.

**Create routines:**

Consistent, simple processes. 

Same steps every time reduces cognitive load.

**Simplify devices:**

**Remove unused apps:**  Reduce clutter and confusion.

**Home screen essentials only:**  Phone, messages, photos, video call.

**Larger text and icons:**  Easier to see and tap.

**Reduce notifications:**  Overwhelming and confusing during decline.

**Automate bill payment:**

Set up automatic payments before memory fails. 

Prevents missed payments, late fees, service interruptions.

**Caregiver access:**

Grant access to trusted family member while you can still: 
- Explain accounts
- Provide passwords
- Authorize them legally
- Teach them what needs to be managed

**Transitional control:**

Early:    You manage with caregiver as backup.  
Middle:  Joint management.   
Late:   Caregiver fully manages.

### Face/Voice Recognition Failures

**Biometric authentication becomes problematic:**

**Facial recognition:**

Disease can change facial features (weight loss, muscle tone, structure).  
Recognition may fail. 

**Voice recognition:**

Speech changes, voice weakens.   
Voice authentication may fail.

**Fingerprint:**

Usually most reliable biometric.

But hand tremors, skin changes can affect recognition.

**Preparation:**

**Don't rely exclusively on biometrics.**

Always have PIN/password backup. 

**Document backup access methods:**

Written down, accessible to caregiver.

**Test periodically:**

Ensure backup methods still work.

**Disable biometrics if they fail:**

Switch to simpler PIN before frustration mounts.

### Digital Power of Attorney

**Standard power of attorney may not cover digital assets.**

**Digital-specific POA includes:**

**Explicit authority over:**
- Email and online accounts
- Social media
- Digital files and cloud storage
- Computers and devices
- Websites and domain names
- Cryptocurrency
- Digital purchases and subscriptions

**Rights to:**
- Access accounts
- Manage, modify, delete content
- Close accounts
- Transfer digital assets
- Act on your behalf online

**Legal recognition:**

Many services require specific legal authority to grant access to another person.

Generic POA may not be sufficient.

**Digital POA provides legal backing** for caregiver to act. 

**Setup:**

**Consult attorney** specializing in elder law or estate planning.

Digital assets are relatively new legal area.   Specialist knowledge helpful.

**Include:**
- Specific list of digital assets and accounts
- Broad authority over digital property
- Duration (immediate, upon incapacity, etc.)
- Successor agents (if primary unable to serve)

**Provide copies to:**
- Agent/caregiver
- Your attorney
- Family members
- Financial institutions (if they require it)

**Update as life changes:**

New accounts, closed accounts, change in chosen agent.

### Common Mistakes

**Waiting too long to prepare**  
"I'm still fine, I'll do it later."   Cognitive decline is insidious.   Prepare early.

**Not simplifying digital life**  
Complex account structures impossible to manage during decline.

**Relying only on biometrics**  
When they fail, no backup access.

**Not granting legal authority early**  
By the time you need caregiver to manage, you may not be capable of granting authority.

**Caregiver not trained**  
You grant access but never teach them how to use accounts.

**No written instructions**  
"I'll explain it to you" but then you can't remember or communicate.

**Assuming family will figure it out**  
Complex digital systems are confusing even for tech-savvy people.

**Not discussing wishes**  
Family doesn't know what you want done with accounts, data, online presence.

**Isolation of information**  
Only you know accounts exist.   Caregiver can't manage what they don't know about.

### Quick Wins

- **1 hour:** Consolidate accounts (close redundant email, banking, storage accounts).
- **30 minutes:** Create simple, illustrated instructions for essential digital tasks.
- **20 minutes:** Grant trusted person access to critical accounts with their knowledge and training. 
- **Variable:** Consult attorney about digital power of attorney. 

*Your digital life outlasts you.    Planning ensures your memories are preserved, your assets protected, and your loved ones spared unnecessary burden.*
